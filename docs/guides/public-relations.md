# Strategic communications

Public communications are handled with care. While internal transparency is always an expection, sharing everything all the time externally can erode necessary trust boundaries. It's with this in mind that we limit public communications to critical milestones, while encouraging transparency and engagement.

## Communications strategy

The strategy applies to communications from EAR participants, Programs, Services, Steering, and shared EAR accounts. It also covers work that participants communicate through their own accounts when they choose to locate it in relation to EAR.

The people closest to an activity, inquiry, or body of knowledge remain responsible for its substance. EAR’s shared accounts and coordination practices provide context, support, and narrative continuity across that work. The strategy doesn’t create a central approval process for ordinary public communication but is anchored in trustful guidelines, peer advice, and collective steering.


## Make EAR’s inquiry, activity, and knowledge understandable

EAR communications need to provide enough context for people to understand what they’re encountering and how they might use it.

Depending on the communication, that may include:

- the condition, question, decision, need, or opportunity being addressed
- what EAR or its participants are doing in relation to it
- who produced the communication and in what capacity
- whether it reflects an individual perspective, a Program, a Service, Steering, or EAR as an organization
- what is known, proposed, disputed, uncertain, or still developing
- which other people, projects, findings, or ecosystem developments are directly relevant
- what the communication is intended to clarify, invite, or support

Every post or conversation won’t need to explain all of this. The amount of context should fit the form and the potential consequences of misunderstanding.

Context allows knowledge to travel beyond the people who produced it. It also lets people follow how questions and interpretations develop, compare perspectives from different parts of developing ecosystems, and understand where their own knowledge may be relevant.

EAR’s communications should also help separate activities add up to a clearer account of the ecosystem dynamics. Narrative coordination can show how research, community experience, technical development, governance, economic conditions, and other areas affect one another without presenting them as one uniform story.

## Invite participation

EAR communications should make clear what kind of attention, relationship, or response is being invited.

Participation can remain light. Someone may read, watch, or listen and bring useful knowledge into their own work. They may ask a question, share a post, or join a public discussion.

Other communications may invite deeper involvement, including:

- contributing experience, evidence, interpretation, or criticism
responding to a research question or developing finding
- joining an event, inquiry, or working conversation
- collaborating with a Program
- requesting support from a Service
- proposing related work
- offering skills, relationships, funding, or other resources
- taking on a defined volunteer, operational, facilitation, or stewardship role

The invitation should fit the purpose and stage of the activity. It should also be clear enough for someone to understand what responding would involve.

Participation doesn’t always lead inward toward EAR. EAR participants also contribute in the public conversations by responding to other people’s work, contributing knowledge, asking questions, amplifying useful activity, and joining conversations already underway.

## Build collective capacity

EAR communications should strengthen the ability of people and groups to investigate shared conditions, develop knowledge, and act together.

Public communication contributes by:

- circulating research, practical learning, and developing interpretations
- making needs, offers, and resource asks visible
- connecting people working on related questions
- helping Programs and Services find participants and collaborators
- supporting fundraising and resource development
- documenting methods and lessons that others can use
making tools, infrastructure, and support easier to discover

Capacity also develops through the cooperation behind public communication.

Participants can help one another clarify ideas, edit writing, prepare for interviews, host conversations, create visual explanations, use unfamiliar media, and understand the wider context around what they want to communicate. Editorial and peer support should be available without becoming mandatory.

## Ground EAR in community

EAR’s communications should be polyvocal first and formally organizational where the communication calls for it.

Participants bring different experiences, responsibilities, relationships, and views of emerging ecosystem dynamics. Those differences are part of EAR’s learning process. People should be able to discuss EAR’s activities through their own accounts and in their own voices while making their relationship to the work clear.

Formal EAR communications have a narrower role. They carry organizational information, shared decisions, commitments, invitations, and context that belongs to EAR as a whole.

EAR’s content strategy should draw from conversations, needs, conflicts, questions, and developments across ecosystem dynamics as much as it draws from activity inside EAR. This keeps communications grounded in relationship and makes public listening part of the research process.

EAR should also model cooperative action in public. That includes showing uncertainty, acknowledging different perspectives, making authorship visible, inviting contribution, and allowing people to see how understanding and action develop over time.


## Voice, authorship, and relationship to EAR

People communicating in relation to EAR should make it possible for readers to understand:

- who is speaking
- what knowledge or responsibility they’re speaking from
- how the communication relates to EAR
- whether they’re expressing a personal interpretation or an organizational position

Authorship should remain visible, including when the EAR account amplifies or republishes work.

Participants don’t need to use uniform language or repeat a shared script. Common positioning and narrative coordination provide continuity while leaving room for different voices and interpretations.

## Personal, project, Program, and EAR accounts

Different accounts serve different purposes.

### Personal accounts
Personal accounts carry an individual’s voice, relationships, interests, and experience. Participants can use them to discuss their work, share developing ideas, respond to public conversations, and locate their activity in relation to EAR.

### Project and Program accounts
Project and Program accounts (should there be any) carry communications tied to a defined body of work. These may include research questions, findings, invitations, updates, documentation, and requests for participation or support.

### EAR accounts
EAR’s shared accounts carry communications that belong to the organization and provide context across the wider body of work.

They may be used to:

- publish organizational information and shared decisions
issue invitations and resource asks
- amplify work from participants, Programs, Services, and collaborators
- connect related communications
- provide context that helps people understand how different activity relates to EAR (or not)
- direct people toward relevant conversations, Programs, Services, and publications

The EAR account should help people navigate the work and discover the people doing it. It shouldn’t replace their voices or claim their work as EAR’s own.

## Media and channels
The form of a communication should follow its purpose, audience, and stage of development.

### Bluesky
Bluesky supports timely observations, questions, updates, invitations, public discussion, amplification, and relationship-building.

It’s likely to be EAR’s main surface for regular social communication across personal, project, Program, and organizational accounts.

### Leaflet and longer publishing
Leaflet and other long-form publishing tools support research notes, analysis, interviews, reflections, guides, documentation, and collaborative publications.

Long-form work may present completed findings or make developing questions and interpretations available for response.

### Images and visual communication
Photographs, diagrams, maps, annotated screenshots, visual summaries, and illustrations can make people, systems, findings, and relationships easier to understand.

Accessibility, consent, attribution, and alt text should be considered as part of producing and publishing visual material.

### Audio, video, and Streamplace
Audio and video support interviews, discussions, demonstrations, public working sessions, documentation, and live learning.

Streamplace can provide a space for live and recorded conversation. Recordings may also lead to transcripts, clips, summaries, or written follow-up when those forms add value.

EAR should remain open to other media as new formats become relevant. The strategy doesn’t require every communication to appear in several forms, but it is known practice that diverse multi-media strategies help information be more accessible and understandable to different people.

## Developing public communications

EAR shouldn’t rely only on participants arriving with completed drafts or major announcements.

The strategy proposes a shared planning space where people can:

- bring forward an activity, question, finding, need, or idea that may benefit from public communication
- identify the people closest to it
- clarify what the communication is meant to accomplish
- consider the most useful form and account
- request or offer support
- identify a next step and a working date
- keep track of what has been published and what response it receives

A calendar view can help maintain momentum and coordinate timing. The calendar wouldn’t establish a posting quota or be treated as a hard schedule.

The group will need to decide how this planning space is tended. The responsibility may be shared, rotate among participants, or sit with particular people for a defined period.

## Consultation

Consultation should be proportional to the goals of communication and its possible side effects

Participants generally won’t need approval to speak from their own work or experience. Consultation becomes important when a communication:

- represents another person or group
- includes personal stories, research participation, or sensitive information
- claims to express an EAR position
- communicates a shared decision
- creates financial, legal, safety, or partnership commitments
may substantially affect people who haven’t been involved in preparing it

Consultation may involve people directly affected, the person responsible for the relevant Program or Service, Steering, or someone with relevant knowledge to the topic being discussed.

Its purpose is to improve judgment, identify concerns, and clarify responsibility. It shouldn’t become routine group approval for ordinary communications.

## Relationship to Programs and Services

Programs and participants determine what they’re investigating, developing, or organizing. Communications begin from those activities and from the ecosystem conditions around them.

Services can contribute according to what a communication requires. For example:

- Editorial may support writing, editing, framing, or publication.
- Convening may support interviews, public discussions, or working sessions.
- Commons may preserve published knowledge and connect it with related work.
- Care may help a public response develop into a relationship with EAR.
- Development may help connect public needs and opportunities with resources.
- Systems may support accounts, tools, access, and technical production.

These are available relationships, rather than required stages that every communication must pass through.
